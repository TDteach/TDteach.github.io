The paper tests whether outcome-reward Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) can recover after that initialization rather than merely reproduce it.</p>
<p>The experiment uses OLMo-2-1B on GSM8K. The authors define six error types from observed model failures, inject them into gold reasoning traces, and vary corruption ratio, error location, and trace-answer consistency across 361 configurations, reaching 100% corruption. A regex-based propagation procedure carries altered values through later equations and final answers; it succeeds only for traces compatible with that format, leaving skipped or implied steps outside the sweep. Each SFT checkpoint is then followed by GRPO on the full uncorrupted GSM8K training set. The primary evaluation is pass@2 on the 1,319-example test set.</p>
<p>The decisive result is asymmetric. Aggregated SFT-only accuracy drops from 43.61% at the gold baseline to 13.80% under full corruption. After GRPO, however, aggregated accuracy stays within 0.5 percentage points of the gold SFT+GRPO baseline across corruption ratios, including full corruption; this is an aggregate statement, not a guarantee for every run. The initialization ablation sharpens the practical lesson: SFT on only eight incorrect, three-line form-only examples reaches 69.30% after GRPO, versus 35.00% for GRPO without SFT. Several other minimal form-only initializations also beat gold-trace initialization, with reported maxima of 9.11 and 8.59 percentage points above the 60.71% gold post-GRPO result. Across the configuration grid, SFT-only accuracy has near-zero, statistically nonsignificant reported correlation with post-GRPO accuracy (r from -0.0631 to 0.0002).</p>
<p>The authors interpret these patterns as SFT establishing response form while RL develops reasoning function, but that mechanism is not independently isolated here. The transferable operation is therefore a controlled budget test: corrupt trace content systematically, hold the verifier-training data fixed, and compare full-trace SFT with tiny structural-only initializations before investing in rationale curation.</p>
<p>Do not generalize the result beyond the tested boundary: the evaluation uses one base model and GSM8K, and the authors say larger models and harder tasks are untested. The six error types cover 93.51% of observed numerical failures rather than all failures. The key question for replication is whether form-only warm starts retain their advantage when the task, model, trace syntax, or verifier changes.</p></div><p class="reading-reason">Read it to decide whether your next post-training experiment should spend annotation budget on exact reasoning traces or first test a minimal structural warm start before verifier-based RL.</p><details class="pn-sources"><summary>Source passages</summary><nav class="sources" aria-label="Source passages"><ul><li><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26756v1" rel="noopener noreferrer">abstract; §3.4 Regex-based propagation; §5.1 SFT degrades, but RL corrects; §4.7 Evaluation; §5.5 SFT before GRPO is still needed; §5.3 Form-only initialization outperforms standard initialization; §5.4 SFT accuracy is weakly associated with post-GRPO performance; Table 1; §1 Introduction; Limitations</a><span class="pn-source-id">arxiv:2611.26756v1</span></li></ul></nav></details></article>
<article class="pn-article" id="paper-2611.26803" aria-labelledby="heading-paper-2611.26803"><h2 id="heading-paper-2611.26803">Saved GRPO Rollouts Support Staged Student Distillation Without an Additional Teacher-Generation Pass in the Reported Settings</h2><p class="paper-title"><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26803v1" rel="noopener noreferrer">Don&#x27;t Discard Your Rollouts: Reusing Teacher RL Traces for Student Distillation</a></p><div class="pn-prose"><p>Canonical distillation normally discards teacher RL traces and regenerates data from the final teacher. That creates a concrete baseline problem: naive SFT on saved rollouts underperforms canonical distillation, while naive DPO is unstable and can collapse on weaker students. The practical question is whether the archive’s own metadata can make reuse selective enough.</p>
<p>The method treats rollout reuse as a data-selection problem using metadata already recorded during GRPO. Prompt pass rates order the archive: the easiest prompts feed an SFT warm start, followed by mixed-success prompts for DPO. For each prompt, cached teacher log-likelihoods serve as a proxy for selecting student-compatible completions. The recipe is evaluated on mathematical reasoning and Python coding tasks with students from multiple model families.</p>
<p>The strongest comparison is conditional. On the reported average maths metric, curated SFT-to-DPO reuse is 0.37 points below canonical synthetic-data distillation, with a 95% confidence interval of [-0.82, +0.08], which includes zero; this is an average across two teachers, five students, and five benchmarks, not parity in every setting. The contrast with naive reuse is informative: random saved-rollout SFT trails canonical distillation in all ten maths teacher–student settings and seven of eight coding comparisons. Cached teacher-likelihood selection improves over random completion selection in the reported maths SFT and DPO comparisons. In one low-support case, the SFT warm start lifts Gemma-2-2B on MATH500 from 4.3% to 17.1% for standalone versus warm-started DPO.</p>
<p>For a GRPO pipeline, the transferable operation is to retain prompt pass rates and per-completion teacher likelihoods, then test the SFT-to-DPO staging against both random selection and regenerated data. Table 2 reports zero new completions and zero response tokens for the recipe in the listed settings, but that count excludes the GRPO generation already performed to create the archive. Finally, keep the boundary explicit: the tested teachers are 4B and 8B and students 1.2B–4B, so transfer across larger capacity gaps remains open; reported evaluation repeats do not replace multiple training seeds.</p></div><p class="reading-reason">Read this if you already run GRPO and want a concrete way to test whether archived traces can replace a separate teacher-generation pass.</p><details class="pn-sources"><summary>Source passages</summary><nav class="sources" aria-label="Source passages"><ul><li><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26803v1" rel="noopener noreferrer">abstract; Section 7.1, Closing the Gap to Canonical Distillation; Appendix B.1; Section 7.2, The Need for Data Selection; Appendix C.1, Table 12; Section 7.3, When Does Preference Learning Help?; Section 7.1, Table 2; Section 8, Limitations</a><span class="pn-source-id">arxiv:2611.26803v1</span></li></ul></nav></details></article>
<article class="pn-article" id="paper-2611.26863" aria-labelledby="heading-paper-2611.26863"><h2 id="heading-paper-2611.26863">Rewarded self-generated false facts become more recallable in tested GRPO setups</h2><p class="paper-title"><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26863v1" rel="noopener noreferrer">LLMs Memorize Their Own Hallucinations During Post-Training</a></p><div class="pn-prose"><p>The concrete limitation is measurement: an outcome reward can accept a rollout for its correct final answer while leaving false intermediate claims unexamined. This study therefore asks whether a false atomic fact generated in such a trace becomes more recallable later, rather than evaluating only the final answer.</p>
<p>The authors sample RL rollouts for knowledge questions, extract atomic false claims from correct-final-answer rollouts, turn each claim into an MCQ, and probe saved checkpoints before and after the claim’s training point. They define memorization as the change in checkpoint-level recall after the fact’s appearance, comparing later recall with recall immediately before the corresponding update. The main HalluSet-train analysis excludes questions for which no correct rollout was generated, so its headline effect is not an estimate for all training questions or incorrect-answer trajectories.</p>
<p>Across six open models from two model families and two architectures, on QuALITY and TriviaQA under GRPO post-training, recall for hallucinated facts drawn from correct-answer training rollouts increased by 4.83 points on average, versus 0.17 points for hallucinations drawn from held-out questions. This is a change in MCQ-probe recall, not a direct estimate of free-form hallucination probability. The paper also reports that models memorized false facts they immediately retracted during reasoning. An incorrect-rollout control generally did not show the same positive memorization pattern, although its scores were not uniformly non-positive on every setting.</p>
<p>The authors report higher memorization scores for hallucinations from harder training questions, but this is a qualitative, figure-dependent trend: the supplied text does not expose the plotted values or their variability. On QuALITY only, a GRPO KL coefficient of 0.001 reduced but did not eliminate the memorization score, with an average reduction of 0.52 points; this does not establish the same behavior on TriviaQA or other configurations. Hallucination extraction, labeling, and MCQ construction rely substantially on GPT-4.1-mini, with the appendix reporting 98% precision on 200 sampled TriviaQA labels rather than validation for every setting.</p>
<p>The transferable operation is to log atomic factual claims during training, attach each claim to its first checkpoint appearance, and retain held-out and incorrect-rollout controls. A useful follow-up is to test whether process-level factual penalties reduce later recall of false claims without reducing correct final answers.</p></div><p class="reading-reason">Read it to borrow the checkpoint-probe design, then test whether its recall signal survives free-form and process-level evaluations.</p><details class="pn-sources"><summary>Source passages</summary><nav class="sources" aria-label="Source passages"><ul><li><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26863v1" rel="noopener noreferrer">abstract; §2.2, [S2.SS2.SSS0.Px3.p2-p3]; §2.1, [S2.SS1.SSS0.Px2.p1-p4]; §3, Table 1 ([S3.T1]); §2.2, [S2.SS2.SSS0.Px2.p1-p2]</a><span class="pn-source-id">arxiv:2611.26863v1</span></li></ul></nav></details></article>
<article class="pn-article" id="paper-2611.27068" aria-labelledby="heading-paper-2611.27068"><h2 id="heading-paper-2611.27068">A fixed decision model supplies zero-shot step credit for agent reinforcement learning</h2><p class="paper-title"><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27068v1" rel="noopener noreferrer">Decision Models Are General-Purpose Process Reward Models</a></p><div class="pn-prose"><p>RL from verifiable outcomes gives a language agent one reward per episode, so every step is credited alike. If every rollout in a group fails, the binary episode outcome cannot rank the steps within those rollouts. DM-PRM addresses this gap by asking a fixed decision model one yes/no progress question per step, converting its answer probability into standardized credit, and adding that credit to the underlying policy-gradient advantage. The approach is zero-shot: it uses no step labels, fine-tuning, or learned critic.</p>
<p>The most useful evidence is conditional rather than universal. In nine early ALFWorld training groups where all eight sampled rollouts failed, the authors report that the step signal still distinguished useful from unproductive actions. In that analysis, steps that explored or interacted with objects were 21% of all steps but 93% of the tenth with the highest credit. This supports the proposed mechanism for supplying an early training signal, but it is an analysis of selected all-failure groups, not a causal intervention.</p>
<p>On the reported benchmark settings, adding DM-PRM to GRPO improved all six ALFWorld task types, the aggregate seen and unseen results, and both WebShop score and success over the paper’s GRPO runs. The signal also improved GiGPO and GraphGPO on aggregate benchmark outcomes, so the paper presents it as a plug-in that complements existing step-level signals rather than replaces them.</p>
<p>The baseline is a central design choice. The paper reports that a pooled WebShop baseline encouraged browsing and buying less; it therefore used a per-action-kind baseline and selected the credit weight with pilot runs. A new environment needs its own question text and may need a baseline per kind. This makes the reusable lesson an experiment-design question: hold the policy-gradient method fixed, compare pooled and action-kind-specific standardization, and inspect both behavior and task outcomes.</p>
<p>The evidence remains bounded. Experiments cover two benchmarks, one 1.5B policy model, and a closed external decision model. The standard WebShop split also has substantial goal overlap—91% of validation goals are training goals for a given training group—and the separate held-out-goal numbers are not comparable to that split. Finally, the scorer receives the known episode outcome in the trajectory log, so its scores are not outcome-independent; the proposed contribution is attribution of that outcome across steps, not an independent progress oracle.</p></div><p class="reading-reason">Read this to see how a queried step signal is inserted into existing policy-gradient advantages—and why the pooled-versus-action-kind baseline changes the experiment you should run.</p><details class="pn-sources"><summary>Source passages</summary><nav class="sources" aria-label="Source passages"><ul><li><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27068v1" rel="noopener noreferrer">abstract; §2, “The decision model as a zero-shot PRM”; §3.3, “Credit when every rollout fails”; App. E.1; Table 1, “gain over GRPO” row; §3.1, “Methods” and “Evaluation”; §3.2, “A plug-in for any policy-gradient method”; Table 1; App. E.1, paragraph preceding Table 10; §5, Conclusion, paragraph 2; §5, Conclusion, paragraph 2; §2, “What a step is compared with”; App. D.2, “Baseline and weight of the credit”; App. C.1, “WebShop: train and test goals”; abstract; §2, “The decision model as a zero-shot PRM”</a><span class="pn-source-id">arxiv:2611.27068v1</span></li></ul></nav></details></article>
<article class="pn-article" id="paper-2611.27227" aria-labelledby="heading-paper-2611.27227"><h2 id="heading-paper-2611.27227">CreditFilter calibrates soft within-response entropy weights for critic-free RLVR</h2><p class="paper-title"><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27227v1" rel="noopener noreferrer">CreditFilter: Calibrated Token Weights over the Within-Response Entropy Rank for RLVR</a></p><div class="pn-prose"><p>Critic-free RLVR typically assigns a uniform advantage to every token. An earlier intervention instead retained policy gradients only on the top 20% highest-entropy positions, but CreditFilter’s motivating criticism is that such a heuristic does not measure a token’s actual impact on the outcome.</p>
<p>CreditFilter formalizes that target as <strong>forking value</strong>: the variance of success probability across the policy’s next-token choices. For on-policy rollouts of a fixed prompt with a binary verifier, the expected forking values along a response sum to the prompt’s reward variance. The factorization separates fork probability, available from the softmax, from fork consequence, which requires costly continuations to estimate.</p>
<p>Instead of estimating consequence for every training token, CreditFilter ranks entropy positions within each response and applies an exponential window over that within-response rank. On Qwen3-8B-Base, the measured fork-probability profile declines approximately exponentially, with fitted widths of 0.111 for correct responses and 0.155 for wrong responses. CreditFilter uses this profile to calibrate its weighting window. After offline calibration, the trainer computes weights from existing rollout and policy quantities, so the rule needs no extra training-time forward passes.</p>
<p>Across three seeds, CreditFilter exceeds DAPO by 7.7 points on MATH500, 10.5 points on AIME24, and 9.1 points on AIME25. The comparison needs a budget qualification: at equal steps, CreditFilter trains on 2.1 times as many tokens because its responses are longer, while the paper’s equal-token comparison is limited to one seed.</p>
<p>A transferable research operation is to derive the desired credit budget first, use continuation sampling to calibrate a cheap proxy, and then ablate the ranking coordinate and decay rate separately. Here, ranking across the batch loses 5–8 points and doubling the calibrated window widths loses 9–15 points, although these ablations are single runs. The proxy also has a clear boundary: softmax fork probability is measured, while fork consequence remains costly to estimate. Training evidence is narrow: all results use one base model and one prompt set, with 30-problem AIME sets and a 20,480-token cap.</p>
<p>Read this paper to learn how a continuation-based measurement can be converted into a no-extra-forward-pass RL rule, while keeping calibration, compute budget, and proxy validity separate.</p></div><p class="reading-reason">Read it to learn how a continuation-based measurement can be converted into a no-extra-forward-pass RL rule, while keeping calibration, compute budget, and proxy validity separate.</p><details class="pn-sources"><summary>Source passages</summary><nav class="sources" aria-label="Source passages"><ul><li><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27227v1" rel="noopener noreferrer">abstract</a><span class="pn-source-id">arxiv:2611.27227v1</span></li><li><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01939v2" rel="noopener noreferrer">Section 5.3, High-entropy tokens drive reinforcement learning for LLM reasoning; Table 2</a><span class="pn-source-id">arxiv:2506.01939v2</span></li></ul></nav></details></article></div>
